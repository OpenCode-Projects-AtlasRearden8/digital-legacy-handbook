# The When-I'm-Gone Folder

*One folder. It holds everything a loved one would need on the worst day.
Build it in one or two sittings, then let it quietly exist — that's the whole
point. You are not writing a memoir; you're leaving a map.*

---

## What it is

A single, clearly labeled folder — a physical one in a drawer or safe, and an
encrypted digital twin if you like — that contains:

- The **handoff letter** ([printable version here](07-handoff-letter.md))
- The **what-I've-got list** ([what to write down](02-what-to-write-down.md))
- Copies or notes on key **paper documents**
- A short **wishes page**
- Instructions for your trusted person

## Choose the physical form

Pick the simplest option you'll actually maintain:

| Option | Setup effort | Security | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A labeled folder in a home desk or filing cabinet | 10 minutes | Low–medium (add a locked drawer) | Most people |
| A fireproof/waterproof document bag or home safe | 20 minutes | High | Anyone with a safe already |
| A safe-deposit box **plus a note of it in the folder** | 1 trip | Very high | Originals like deeds |

**Pick one, write its location on the handoff letter.** Never split the truth
across "a little of here, a little of there" — one folder is the rule.

## The encrypted digital twin (optional but smart)

Your digital twin is a copy for when you travel or move, and it must be
encrypted. Two local-first ways that need no special software:

1. **Password-protected ZIP** — `zip -e legacy.zip folder/` on any computer.
   Send the password to your trusted person by voice or in person — never in
   the same channel as the file.
2. **Password manager secure notes** — most managers have a "Secure Note" or
   "Legacy/Emergency access" feature. Legacy contact features are ideal.

Never: an unencrypted USB stick, a plain text file on your desktop, or an
 emailed attachment sitting in your sent folder for years.

## What goes in (the master list)

Work to this checklist — tick as you go:

- [ ] **Handoff letter**, signed, one page
      ([printable](07-handoff-letter.md))
- [ ] **Location key** — where original documents live (deed, car title, will)
- [ ] **Contact list** — the 5–10 people/ offices that matter (see below)
- [ ] **Accounts list** — from [what to write down](02-what-to-write-down.md),
      noting *where passwords actually live* (manager, phone, paper vault)
- [ ] **Recurring charges list** — subscriptions and autopays, with what
      service each is and whether to cancel or continue
- [ ] **Wishes page** — [memorialize or delete choices](04-memorialize-or-delete.md)
- [ ] **Paper copies (or notes about where they are)**: ID, insurance cards,
      will or trust papers, advance directive if you have one
- [ ] **Small business / side income notes** if any — who to tell, what to
      pause, logins' *location* not the logins
- [ ] **Review log** — the last page: simply the date and "checked by" each year

## The 10-minute starter version

Too much to do today? Do only this:

1. Grab any folder or envelope. Write on it: **"IN CASE OF EMERGENCY — open
   with your name and today's date inside."**
2. Start or print the handoff letter and sign it.
3. Add one page: your three most important things (usually: phone passcode
   lives here, bank is at X, family knows Y).
4. Tell your trusted person it exists and where it is.

That's already 80% of the value. Everything else in this handbook makes the
remaining 20% easier over time.

## Review once a year

Calendar reminder: *"Digital legacy review — 20 minutes."* Walk the folder,
update anything stale, add the year to the review log. That's it.
